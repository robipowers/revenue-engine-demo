The single most important thing to understand about this job

There is a war inside every RevOps team between deterministic and probabilistic logic.

Deterministic = a rule. “If country is Germany, route to EMEA.” Same input, same output, forever. Auditable, boring, reliable.

Probabilistic = a model or an LLM. “This lead seems like it wants pricing.” Handles mess a rule can't, but can be wrong and can't easily be explained.

LeanData's JD asks for “blending probabilistic AI reasoning with deterministic logic.” That word blending is the job. Your demo is a working answer to exactly that sentence — and the answer is: let AI judge, but never let it be the last word, and write down which one decided.

The four responsibilities, and what each really asks

  1. 1. “Architect the GTM Intelligence Foundation — a unified data layer that merges structured (DataLake/APIs/MCPs) and unstructured signals (Calls/Emails/Chats) for agentic reasoning.”

    What they're asking: can you build one queryable place that holds both the tidy stuff (rows in a database) and the messy stuff (what someone said in an email), and expose it so an AI can use it?

    What Relay does: a DuckDB warehouse holds the structured side — accounts, contacts, activities, opportunities, joined by keys. The unstructured side is the free-text notes field on every inbound lead, which gets read and classified. An MCP server exposes the warehouse as three tools any AI agent can call. That is literally “DataLake / APIs / MCPs.”

  2. 2. “Engineer Agentic Workflows — autonomous systems in the execution path of the funnel, blending probabilistic AI reasoning with deterministic logic.”

    What they're asking: not a chatbot on the side. Something that sits in the path — if it breaks, leads stop moving.

    What Relay does: a seven-stage pipeline where two stages are LLM judgment calls and five are deterministic. Every LLM stage has a deterministic fallback, and when the fallback fires it is recorded. Right now, with no API key set, 15.6% of all decisions in the log were fallbacks — and the system still completed every run.

  3. 3. “Lead predictive modeling — own the logic and technical setup for scoring and propensity models, integrated into the agentic orchestration layer.”

    What they're asking: can you build a scoring model and wire it into the thing that acts on it? Many people can do one or the other.

    What Relay does: a logistic regression on six warehouse-derived features, holdout AUC 0.827, and its output directly sets priority and SLA. Score ≥ 0.7 → P1, 15-minute SLA. Below → P2, 4-hour SLA. The model is not a report; it changes what happens to the lead.

  4. 4. “Prototype and Scale GTM Innovation — rapidly build and test AI-native concepts and turn successes into SOP.”

    What they're asking: speed, and the judgment to know which prototypes deserve to become permanent.

    What Relay does: it is the prototype. The thing that makes it SOP-ready rather than a toy is the audit log and the human approval gate — the two features that let an organisation actually trust it in production.

What Relay actually is

A lead arrives. The system matches it to an account, enriches it from a warehouse, judges intent, scores it with a trained model, routes it to a rep, and stops for a human — writing an unforgeable record of every step.

That sentence is the whole product. Everything else is detail.

The mental model

A mail room with a paper trail. A letter arrives with no address. A clerk works out which company it's for, pulls their file, reads what they want, checks how hot they are, decides who handles it — then puts it in an out-tray for a supervisor instead of delivering it. Every step gets stamped into a logbook that can only be added to.

Why build this instead of talking about it

The JD says “we aren't just looking for someone to manage tools, we want a builder.” Every other candidate will say they're a builder. The demo is the only form of that claim that can't be faked in an interview.

But there's a second, sharper reason, and it's the one to lead with:

Say: “The hard part of agentic GTM isn't making the AI work. It's making it trustworthy enough that a VP will let it touch the funnel. So I built the governance first: every decision is logged with a hash of its inputs, every AI step has a deterministic fallback, and no assignment is ever written without a human approving it. The AI is the easy half.”

That reframe does three things at once: it shows you understand their actual blocker, it separates you from candidates demoing a chatbot, and it makes the boring parts of your build (audit log, HITL gate, fallbacks) into the headline instead of the footnotes.

Architecture in one picture

data/inbound/*.json ← inbound signal (structured + free text) │ 1 MATCH rapidfuzz, 3-band policy ──→ 2 TIE-BREAK LLM, or deterministic fallback │ 3 ENRICH DuckDB star schema: accounts · contacts · activities · opportunities │ 4 INTENT LLM summary, or keyword fallback │ 5 SCORE logistic regression, features computed as of the scoring clock │ 6 ROUTE territory map → availability-aware round-robin → SLA tier │ 7 HITL GATE ──→ approve / reject every stage → runs/audit.jsonl

Two of those seven stages are probabilistic (2 and 4). Five are deterministic. That ratio is the “blending” the JD asks for, and you can point at it on screen.

The seven stages, in depth

1 · Match — which account is this lead?

An inbound lead says “Hine Metric Co.” Your warehouse has “Hivemetric” and “Pinemetric.” Which is it?

Relay tries the cheap, certain thing first: if the lead has a domain and that domain exactly matches an account, done — confidence 100, method domain_exact. No fuzziness, no AI.

If there's no domain, it falls back to fuzzy name matching with rapidfuzz (a fast string-similarity library) and applies a three-band policy:

ScoreBandWhat happens
≥ 90AUTO-MATCHConfident. Proceed.
70 – 89AMBIGUOUSEscalate to the tie-break stage. This is where AI earns its keep.
< 70NO MATCHCreate a new account, route to manual review. Never guess.

Why three bands and not one threshold: a single cutoff forces every borderline case into a wrong answer. Three bands create a middle zone where the system says “I'm not sure” — and being able to say that is what makes the confident answers believable.

2 · Tie-break — the first AI judgment call

“Hine Metric Co” scores 85.7 against both Hivemetric and Pinemetric. A rule can't break that tie sensibly; a human would look at it and think. That is exactly the shape of problem an LLM is good at.

So Relay asks an LLM to pick — but with a hard guardrail:

Guardrail: the LLM MUST answer with a name from the candidate list.
Anything else (out-of-list, error, timeout, empty) → deterministic fallback.

The fallback sorts by (-score, account_id) — highest score wins, and when scores tie exactly, the lowest account id breaks it. In the demo both candidates sit at exactly 85.7, so account id is what actually decides, and the console says so in those words.

This is the most interview-valuable thirty seconds in your whole demo. It shows an LLM in the execution path, a guardrail that constrains its output to a valid set, a deterministic fallback when it can't answer, and an audit record that says which one decided. That is the entire JD in one stage.

3 · Enrich — pull the account's file

Deterministic. Queries the warehouse for the matched account's contacts, recent activities, and open opportunities. No AI, no ambiguity — this is a join.

Worth knowing: the enrichment is where “context layer” becomes concrete. The lead arrived with seven fields. After enrichment the system knows the account's industry, size, territory, who works there, what they've been doing, and what's in the pipeline.

4 · Intent — the second AI judgment call

The lead's note reads: “hey -- we're re-evaluating vendors,, budget already approved!! need pricing + integration details asap b4 end of Q.”

An LLM classifies that into one of five labels and writes a short summary. The guardrails: the label must be from the allowed list, the summary must be ≤20 words and ≤200 characters, and control characters are stripped. Fail any check → deterministic keyword fallback.

The keyword fallback scans for terms in priority order — pricing/price/cost/quote/budget → pricing_inquiry; demo/trial/walkthrough/meeting → demo_request; evaluat/compar/vendor/migration → evaluation; and so on.

Know this cold: the summary is model-authored free text. The code comments say renderers must treat it as untrusted. That's prompt-injection awareness — a lead could write “ignore previous instructions” in their note. Mentioning this unprompted signals a level of care most candidates won't show.

5 · Score — the propensity model

Six features, computed as of a fixed timestamp, fed to a trained logistic regression. Covered in depth below — this is the stage most likely to get drilled.

6 · Route — territory, availability, SLA

Fully deterministic, three steps:

  1. Territory. Country → territory via an explicit map. United States splits by state: 27 eastern states → AMER-East, everything else → AMER-West. Germany → EMEA. Japan → APAC.
  2. Round-robin with availability. Walk the territory's rep roster from the stored rotation cursor. If a rep is unavailable, skip them — and write a routing_skip audit record saying who was skipped and why. In the demo Priya Raman is on vacation; she has 0 routed and 9 skipped.
  3. SLA tier. Propensity ≥ 0.7 → P1, 15 minutes. Below → P2, 240 minutes.

Why the skip being audited matters: in most routing systems, a skip is invisible — the lead just lands somewhere and nobody knows why. When a VP asks “why didn't Priya get that lead,” Relay has the answer as a record. That's the difference between automation and accountable automation.

7 · HITL gate — the stop

HITL = Human In The Loop. The pipeline computes a full proposal, writes it to runs/pending/<lead_id>.json, and stops. Nothing is assigned.

A later approve or reject consumes that stored proposal exactly as it was shown — it does not re-run the pipeline and does not advance the round-robin a second time. If the pending file is corrupt, the run fails closed: the discard is audited and the run stops, so an unseen proposal can never be auto-applied.

That failure is called train/serve skew, and putting both behind one function is how you make it impossible rather than merely unlikely.

The propensity model, in depth

This is the stage most likely to get drilled, because responsibility #3 is “lead our predictive modeling efforts.”

What it predicts

Given an account's engagement before an opportunity was created, how likely was that opportunity to be won? Trained on 60 labelled rows (30 won, 30 lost), 42 train / 18 holdout.

The six features and their trained weights

FeatureWeightReading
activity_count_30d+1.8313Engagement volume in the 30 days before the as-of date. Dominant signal by a wide margin.
prior_opportunity_count+0.7530Prior deals with this account. A relationship history helps.
industry_fit−0.5805Whether the industry is in the product's sweet spot. Negative — counter-intuitive. See below.
days_since_last_activity−0.5572Recency. More days since last touch → lower propensity. Sign is intuitive.
employee_band−0.3265Company size as an ordinal.
distinct_contacts_engaged+0.2010Multi-threading. More people engaged → better, but weakly.
intercept −0.1488 · holdout AUC 0.827

Be ready for: “why is industry_fit negative?”

This is the sharpest question available and you should welcome it. The honest answer: on 60 rows of generated data, a weak feature can easily pick up the wrong sign — that is what a small sample does. It's a real property of the fitted model, not something to explain away. The right response is “that's a sign the feature isn't carrying real signal at this sample size, and in production I'd want either more data or that feature dropped.” Saying that demonstrates you read your own model output rather than shipping it blind.

Why AUC 0.827 and not 0.99

AUC = Area Under the ROC Curve. Plain reading: given one won deal and one lost deal at random, how often does the model rank the won one higher? 0.5 is a coin flip. 1.0 is perfect.

0.827 is good. 0.99 would be a red flag — on 60 rows, near-perfect separation almost always means leakage: the model saw something it wouldn't have at prediction time. The demo has a test that enforces the band: 0.65 < AUC < 0.99. A test that fails when your model looks too good is an unusual and very defensible thing to have written.

The scoring clock — the leakage guard

Every feature is computed as of a fixed timestamp (currently 2026-06-17 19:08), derived as the newest opportunity's creation date plus one hour.

Training rows use each opportunity's own created_at — so only activity in the 30 days before that opportunity existed counts. Live scoring uses the same function.

Crucially, the warehouse deliberately contains facts after the clock that the model is not allowed to see. Those rows are chipped post-clock in the Opportunities table.

Say: “If nothing existed after the scoring clock, the guard would be meaningless — you can't prove you're not peeking if there's nothing to peek at. The later data is there on purpose, and the UI marks it.”

Explainability

For any score, the console shows every feature's contribution: coefficient × standardized feature value, the exact terms the model summed, ending in sigmoid(intercept + Σ contributions). The arithmetic closes to the score displayed everywhere else in the app.

The governance layer

The audit log

One append-only JSONL file. One line per decision. Each record carries:

{
  "ts":            "2026-08-06T22:20:53+00:00",
  "stage":         "routing_skip",
  "lead_id":       "L-1001",
  "inputs":        { "territory": "EMEA", "rep": "Priya Raman" },
  "inputs_sha256": "fdd0b8cc088e2a3f...",
  "decision":      { "skipped_rep": "Priya Raman", "reason": "rep_unavailable" },
  "rule_fired":    "skip_unavailable_rep",
  "fallback_used": false
}

Twelve stage types can be emitted, including the unhappy paths: llm_unavailable, pending_discarded, pipeline_error, routed_to_review.

Why the input hash is the clever part

Anyone can log “I assigned this lead to Tomas.” The SHA-256 of the inputs lets you prove what the system was looking at when it decided. Same hash + same rule = same decision, reproducibly. That turns the log from a diary into evidence. If two records share an input hash and a rule, that's a determinism proof, not a duplicate.

The three guarantees, in the order they matter

  1. Append-only. Nothing in the codebase can truncate or rewrite the log.
  2. Fail closed. A corrupt pending proposal is discarded, audited, and the run stops. It is never auto-applied.
  3. No silent AI. Every fallback sets fallback_used: true. You can always tell whether an AI or a rule produced an answer.

The MCP context layer

MCP = Model Context Protocol. An open standard for exposing data and tools to AI agents in a way any compatible client can consume. Think of it as USB for AI context: instead of writing a bespoke integration per model, you expose a tool once and any MCP client can call it.

The JD names MCPs explicitly in responsibility #1, so this is not optional vocabulary.

Relay exposes three read tools:

ToolReturns
lookup_account(query)Ranked match candidates with scores and the policy thresholds — the same scoring code the pipeline's matcher uses, not a reimplementation.
account_context(account_id)The full account file plus its current propensity score.
territory_rules()Rep rosters, territory maps and SLA tiers — the config the router reads at decision time.

Say: “The MCP server isn't a second implementation — it calls the same matcher the pipeline calls. An agent asking ‘which account is this' gets the identical answer the routing engine would get. That's what makes it a context layer rather than a reporting API.”

Questions they'll ask, and how the demo answers

“Walk me through what you built.”

Use the one-sentence version, then the mail-room analogy, then offer the screen. Don't start with the tech stack — start with what it does for a revenue team.

“A lead arrives. The system matches it to an account, enriches it from a warehouse, judges intent, scores it with a trained model, routes it to a rep, and stops for a human — writing an unforgeable record of every step.”

“Is any of this real, or is it mock data?”

Answer directly, no hedging: the sample database is generated, like any demo's. What's real is everything downstream — every score, routing decision and audit record comes from actually running the pipeline. Nothing is typed in.

Then go further, because this is a strength: the lead generator verifies each generated lead against the real matcher before keeping it, so the advertised path mix is measured rather than assumed.

“What happens when the LLM is down?”

It already is — there's no API key set in the demo. Every AI step has a deterministic fallback, the fallback is recorded with fallback_used: true, and the run completes. That's what the 15.6% counts.

The stronger version: “I built the failure path first, because a system that only works when the LLM is up can't sit in the execution path of a funnel.”

“How do you know your model isn't overfit?”

Three answers, in order of strength: (1) holdout AUC 0.827, not 0.99 — near-perfect separation on 60 rows would mean leakage; (2) there's a test that fails if AUC exceeds 0.99; (3) features are computed as-of a fixed clock through the same code path in training and serving, so train/serve skew is structurally impossible rather than just unlikely.

“Why logistic regression and not something modern?”

Because with 60 labelled rows, a gradient-boosted model would fit noise. And because coefficients are directly inspectable — you can show a VP exactly why an account scored what it scored, which matters more than a point of AUC when you're asking someone to trust automated routing.

Then flag the upgrade path honestly: with real volume you'd revisit, and you'd want calibration, not just ranking.

“How would this scale to our actual volume?”

Be honest about what changes. DuckDB is single-node — at real volume the warehouse becomes Snowflake or BigQuery, but the feature code doesn't change because it's already isolated. The pipeline is stateless per lead, so it parallelises. The append-only audit log becomes a real event store. The HITL gate becomes a queue with an SLA on the approver, not just on the rep.

What does not change: the three-band matching policy, the fallback discipline, and the audit contract. Say which parts survive scale and which don't — that's the answer of someone who's thought about production.

“What would you do in your first 90 days here?”

Don't pitch a rebuild. Say you'd map the existing decision points first — where does a lead currently stop moving, and who has to touch it — then instrument before automating: you can't prove an agentic workflow helped if you never measured the manual one. Then pick the highest-friction, most-auditable decision and automate that one end to end, with the governance in place from day one.

This mirrors the JD's “simplify and automate rather than add complexity.”

“What's the hardest part of agentic GTM?”

The reframe from the Why section. Not making the AI work — making it trustworthy enough that a VP lets it touch the funnel. Then point at the audit log, the fallbacks and the HITL gate as the three things that buy that trust.

“Tell me about something in the build you got wrong.”

Have a real one ready — it's a credibility question, not a trap. A true one from this build: the console's routing view initially printed each rep's territory totals on their own row, so the visible column summed to double its own footer. It was caught by review, and the fix was to derive per-rep share honestly rather than delete the column.

Or the deeper one: deal amounts and the model's signal were drawing from the same random stream, so a cosmetic pricing change silently swung holdout AUC between 0.605 and 0.901. Numbers were moving for reasons unrelated to the data's meaning. Splitting them into separate generators fixed it. That story shows you notice when a number moves for the wrong reason.

Quiz yourself

Answer out loud before opening each one. If you can't answer eight of these cleanly, re-read the relevant section rather than the whole guide.

1. What are the three bands in the matching policy, and why three instead of one threshold?

≥90 auto-match, 70–89 ambiguous, <70 no-match. Three bands create a middle zone where the system can say “I'm not sure” and escalate. A single cutoff forces every borderline case into a confident wrong answer — and being able to admit uncertainty is what makes the confident answers believable.

2. An LLM returns a company name that isn't in the candidate list. What happens?

The guardrail rejects it and the deterministic fallback runs: sort by (-score, account_id), take the first. The audit record shows fallback_used: true with rule fallback_highest_score. The LLM never gets to invent an account.

3. Both tie-break candidates score exactly 85.7. What actually decides?

The account id — lowest wins. The score doesn't break the tie because it's identical. This matters: the console says “tied at 85.7 → lowest account id” rather than the vaguer “highest score,” because the vaguer version would be contradicted by the two equal numbers on screen.

4. What is the scoring clock and why does data exist after it?

A fixed timestamp that all features are computed as of — a leakage guard. Data exists after it deliberately: if nothing did, the guard would be unfalsifiable. Opportunities closing after it are chipped post-clock to make the boundary visible.

5. Why would an AUC of 0.99 be bad news?

On 60 rows, near-perfect separation almost always means leakage — the model saw something it wouldn't have at prediction time. There's a test that fails if AUC exceeds 0.99, which is an unusual thing to write and a very defensible one.

6. Which two of the seven stages are probabilistic, and what happens if both fail?

Tie-break (stage 2) and intent (stage 4). If both fail, both fall back to deterministic rules — highest-score-then-lowest-id for the tie-break, keyword scanning for intent — and the run completes normally with two fallback_used: true records. Nothing stalls.

7. What does the input hash in each audit record buy you?

Proof of what the system was looking at when it decided, not just what it decided. Same hash plus same rule means the same decision, reproducibly. It turns the log from a diary into evidence — and two records sharing a hash is a determinism proof, not a duplicate.

8. A rep is on vacation. What does the system do, and why does that matter?

The round-robin skips them and writes a routing_skip audit record naming who was skipped and why. It matters because in most routing systems a skip is invisible — the lead just lands somewhere and nobody can say why. Here, when a VP asks “why didn't Priya get that lead,” there's a record.

9. Why is feature engineering in its own module?

So training and live scoring compute features through the identical code path. If they diverge you get train/serve skew: great test scores, bad production performance. Sharing one function makes that failure structurally impossible rather than merely unlikely.

10. What is MCP and why does it appear in the JD?

Model Context Protocol — an open standard for exposing data and tools to AI agents, so you expose a tool once and any compatible client can call it. It's in responsibility #1 because LeanData wants a context layer any agent can consume, not a bespoke integration per model. Relay exposes three read tools, and lookup_account calls the same matcher the pipeline uses.

11. Nothing gets assigned automatically. Isn't that the opposite of "autonomous"?

This is the best question on the list and you should have a crisp answer. Autonomy is about the reasoning being automated, not the accountability. The system does all the work — matching, enrichment, judgment, scoring, routing — and stops at the one point where a human's signature has value. The gate is configurable; the discipline is that the default is safe. An organisation adopts automation by trusting it incrementally, and you can't earn that trust if the first version can write to the CRM unsupervised.

12. What is LeanData's core product, and how does your demo relate to it?

Lead-to-account matching and routing. Stages 1, 2 and 6 are that problem — three-band matching, an AI tie-break, and availability-aware round-robin with territory rules. You didn't build something adjacent to their product; you built their core problem and added a governance layer on top.
